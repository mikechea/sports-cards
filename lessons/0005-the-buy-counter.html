Lesson 5 · ~10 minutes

The Buy Counter

A customer drops a shoebox on the counter and says "what'll you give me?" Here's how to answer well.

CardVault stores let fans buy, sell, trade, and grade cards.[1] Selling and trading means somebody behind the counter has to look at a stranger's cards, keep them safe, put a number on them, and say why. Do it well and the customer leaves feeling respected even if they hoped for more. Do it badly and you lose the customer, or the shop's money.

The whole job is four steps, done in order.

The counter flow

1 · SORT

Split the pile into hits (worth pricing card by card) and bulk (priced as a lot). Use the four value levers to sort fast.

2 · PROTECT

Handle by the edges. Sleeve anything that might matter before you look closer. It's the customer's card until you've paid for it.

3 · OFFER

Start from sold comps for the same card in the same condition (Lesson 3), then come down to cover the shop's costs and risk.

4 · EXPLAIN

Show the comps, say why the offer is below them, and offer the cash vs. credit choice. Being open about it builds trust.

1 · Sort: hits vs. bulk

Don't price 800 cards one at a time. Run the Lesson 1 levers as a filter and pull anything that trips one: rookie cards of good players, serial-numbered parallels, autos and patches, and anything already graded. Everything else, mostly base cards of non-stars, is bulk. Shops pay a higher share for cards that sell fast and "much less" for slow movers and bulk.[2] Comps are for the hits. Bulk gets one price for the whole lot.

2 · Protect: the supply ladder

A good rule of thumb: "sleeve everything, toploader the keepers, one-touch the few that earn it."[3] Holding cards by the edges, with clean hands, matters too. Fingerprints and corner dings take value off a card, and on your shift that's the store's problem.

3 · Offer: why it's below comps

Comps tell you what a card sells for. The shop still has to do the selling. Say a card has sold for about $100 recently, same card and same grade:

  • Fees. If it sells on eBay, the final value fee for sports cards is 13.25% plus a $0.40 per-order fee on orders over $10, so roughly $13.65 of that $100 is gone.[5] Selling in-store means paying rent and staff instead.
  • Holding time and risk. The card might sit for weeks, and the price can fall while it does. The discount covers overhead and the risk of holding inventory. The seller gets paid right away, with no fees.[2]
  • Profit. The shop has to make money on the resale.

How far below? There's no industry standard, and I couldn't find a survey. These are data points, not rules:

  • A Pennsylvania shop owner wrote that his store pays about 20% under recent comps when buying to resell. He added that other shops may pay less because of higher overhead.[6]
  • A collector quoted by Sports Collectors Digest said some dealers offer only 50–60% of comps because of overhead.[7]
  • The share goes up for cards that sell fast and down for slow movers and bulk.[2]
CardVault's numbers are CardVault's

I found no public source for what CardVault pays, so don't walk in quoting a percentage. In an interview, the strong answer is the reasoning: "I'd start from recent sold comps for the same card and grade, then follow the store's buy guidelines, which account for fees, holding time, and risk."

Cash vs. store credit

Shops usually offer more in credit or trade than in cash.[2] The reason is simple: "Cash leaves the shop; credit comes back." Credit gets spent on the shop's own stock, which carries a normal markup, so the real cost to the shop is lower than the number on the slip.[8] Pokémon bridge: if you've seen a game store's buylist with separate cash and credit columns, it's the same idea. That source's example: a $10 card gets $5 cash or $6.50 credit.[8]

Red flags (beginner level)

You won't be an authenticator, and you don't need to be. Your job is to notice, pause, and get a second opinion. Don't accuse anyone.

  • Counterfeits and reprints. The card stock feels too thin, flimsy, or stiff. The print looks blurry or has inkjet lines. Chrome feels textured or plasticky instead of smooth like glass. Fake Silver Prizms are common, and their silver tone is "almost always wrong." Compare with a copy you know is real.[9]
  • Altered autos and patches. Watch for sticker autos with lifted edges, swapped patches, or relic windows that look re-glued.[9]
  • Trimmed cards. Look for uneven edges, odd corners, or a size difference against a known copy. Trimming is "extremely difficult to detect without equipment."[9] PSA won't give a number grade to a card that shows evidence of trimming, recoloring, or restoration.[10] A trimmed raw card can't become a PSA 10.
  • Fake or tampered slabs. Look up the cert number on PSA's site,[11] then check that the card in the slab matches the record. PSA warns that counterfeiters sometimes print fake labels using real cert numbers. PSA also calls its holder "tamper-evident," which is not tamper-proof. Its buyer's guide says to look for frosting along the edges, a case that flexes, glue or pry marks, and a label that fails a bright-light test.[12]
  • Price too good to be true. Someone happy to take far less than comps for a big card deserves a closer look.[13]

ID and paperwork: California runs a statewide electronic reporting system (CAPSS) for secondhand dealers.[14] I couldn't confirm whether it covers trading card shops, so I'm not teaching a rule here. Ask how the store handles ID when you're hired.

4 · Explain: say it out loud

A script that works

"Here's what this one has actually sold for lately in this condition." (Turn the screen around.) "We have to resell it, which means fees and time, and the price can move while it sits, so our cash offer is $X. If you'd rather take store credit, it's $Y. And these," (the bulk stack) "we'd take as a lot for $Z."

Sold comps, a short honest reason, and a choice. People push back much less on a number they can see the reasoning for.

Real-world rep (20 minutes)

Pick three cards you own: one bulk card, one decent rookie, and your best card. Look up sold comps for each on 130point. Then take them to a local shop or card show table and ask, "What would you offer, cash and credit?" (you don't have to sell). Write down each offer as a percentage of comps, and pay attention to how the buyer explained it. That's free training for the job.

Primary reading

"PSA Security: A Buyer's Guide", PSA. The grading company's own guide to its holder and label security features and the physical signs of a tampered or fake slab. Graded cards cross the counter every day, so this is the red-flag skill most worth learning properly. Open it in a regular browser (it blocks automated readers).

Something unclear, or want to argue with an answer? Ask me in the terminal. Questions like "how would I price a whole binder?" or "what if the customer says another shop offered more?" are exactly what to bring.
