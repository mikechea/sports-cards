Lesson 6 · ~10 minutes

Key Holder Operations

Open the store, run the drawer, close it out balanced, and read the day's numbers.

The card knowledge gets you noticed. The Key Holder part is what the store trusts you with. The CardVault posting lists cash handling, reconciling drawers and safes, opening and closing to company standard, and retail KPIs.[1] In general retail, a key holder is the person who opens and closes the store, arms and disarms the alarm, keeps the codes safe, and stands in for the manager when needed.[2] CardVault's own checklists will be its own. This lesson covers the standard practice they're built on.

Open and close: the shape of the shift

OpenClose
Check the outside before you go in. Broken glass or an open door means don't enter. Get somewhere safe and call the police.[3] Clear the store, lock the doors, and check bathrooms and back areas for anyone still inside.[3]
Go in with two people if you can, lock the door behind you, disarm the alarm, and confirm the cameras are on.[3] Count cash at a set time in a secure room, away from the floor and the front door. Put the drawers in the safe.[3]
Start the POS and count each drawer's starting bank against what it should be.[4] Settle the card batch, close the register session, and prepare the deposit.[3][4]
Huddle: today's sales goal, yesterday's numbers, active promotions.[3] Leave a handover log for the opener. Arm the alarm and check the door is locked, with two people present.[3]

Reconciling a drawer

Every drawer starts with a fixed starting bank (also called the float): enough small bills and coins to make change. Keep it as small as your sales history allows.[4] At the close, one formula tells you what should be in the drawer:

The drawer formula

Expected = starting bank + cash sales − cash refunds − safe drops and paid-outs + paid-ins[5]
Over/short = counted − expected.  Deposit = counted − the bank you leave for the next shift.[4]

Five habits make the number trustworthy:

  • Count blind. Count before you look at the POS expected total, so the number can't steer you.[4][5]
  • Drop excess cash into the safe during the shift so less sits at the register. Log every drop, because it's part of the reconciliation.[4]
  • Overs are as bad as shorts. A drawer that keeps coming up over can mean customers are being shortchanged.[5] Either way: recount by denomination, check voids, refunds, and drops, then write it down.[4]
  • Second pair of eyes. A manager checks the count, and a different person recounts big discrepancies.[4][3] Keep an over/short log for each cashier and have a supervisor review it. Safe access stays limited to a few people.[6]
  • Know the threshold. There's no universal one. Small shops often set $5–10 for a same-day recount and sign-off.[5] Learn CardVault's number, don't guess it.

Card-shop twist: a card shop also buys cards across the counter (Lesson 5). If a buy is ever paid from the drawer, it's a paid-out. Log it, or the drawer will look short by exactly that amount.

In Shopify POS

Shopify tracks this as a register session. You open it by entering the starting amount in the drawer. During the shift, Add cash and Remove cash record movements like safe drops, with a reason code if the store requires one. At close you enter the counted cash, it's compared with the expected balance, and you leave the float in the drawer.[7] A closed session can't be reopened or edited, so count before you confirm.[8] On POS Pro locations, the admin's cash tracking report shows an opening discrepancy (last close vs. this open) and a closing discrepancy (expected vs. counted).[9] Whether CardVault actually runs Shopify isn't confirmed. The posting only calls it "a plus."[1]

Try it: count this drawer

Opening bank was $150. The POS shows $862.50 in cash sales, no refunds, and one $300 safe drop (already filled in below). Your count: 3 × $50, 20 × $20, 8 × $10, 10 × $5, 47 × $1, and 22 quarters. Enter the count before you press Compare.

You should land $20 over. That's not a bonus. Most likely a customer got $20 too little change, or a sale was rung wrong. Log it and flag it, just like a short.

Reading the daily numbers

KPIFormulaWhat it tells you
% to goalactual sales ÷ sales goal × 100Are we on pace today?
Conversiontransactions ÷ visitors × 100[10][11]How many people who walked in actually bought
ATV (avg. transaction value)total sales ÷ transactions[10]How big the average sale was
UPT (units per transaction)units sold ÷ transactions[10][11]How many items each buyer took home
Sales per hoursales ÷ hours. Labor hours (SPLH) or hours open, depending on the store[10]Which hours are busy, and staffing

Conversion and ATV pull in opposite directions at a card shop. A walk-in tourist who buys one $5 pack lifts conversion but drags down ATV. One $2,000 slab does the reverse. UPT is the add-on habit: in Pokémon terms, it's the customer who came in for an Elite Trainer Box and leaves with sleeves and a binder too. Read the three together, and ask what your store's "sales per hour" report actually divides by.

Protecting the case

High-value cards are small, liquid, and easy to switch. The tightest standard comes from jewelry retail: help one customer at a time, show one item at a time, lock the showcase right after taking something out or putting it back, and keep the keys on your wrist, never on the counter.[12] Card sellers say the same thing: expensive cards stay behind glass and get handed out one at a time, while cameras watch the floor.[13] After a 2026 wave of Pokémon break-ins, some Canadian card shops started emptying their cases overnight and keeping top stock in safes or bank boxes.[14] I couldn't find a published CardVault policy on this, so treat it as typical high-value retail practice.
