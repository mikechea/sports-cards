Lesson 4 · ~10 minutes

Pre-Grading a Raw Card

Estimate the grade before PSA does, then decide if the slab is worth the fee.

CardVault stores let customers buy, sell, trade, and grade cards.[1] So the question you'll hear constantly is: "Should I send this in?" A good answer has two halves. First, a realistic grade range from looking at the card. Second, the math: does the likely graded value beat the raw value plus the fees?

What the grader looks at

PSA grades on a 1–10 scale and judges four things.[2] Check them in this order, because centering is the only one you can measure with a ruler.

1 · CENTERING

How even the borders are, left/right and top/bottom, on the front and back. Written as a ratio like 60/40.

2 · CORNERS

A PSA 10 needs four perfectly sharp corners. "The slightest fraying at one or two corners" is already PSA 8 territory.[2]

3 · EDGES

Look for white chipping along the colored border, which shows up badly on dark-bordered chrome cards.

4 · SURFACE

Tilt the card under a light: scratches, print lines, dimples, stains, lost gloss. A 10 needs full original gloss.[2]

The weakest-link rule

PSA's grade definitions read as ceilings. A Mint 9 is allowed only one minor flaw.[2] Perfect centering can't rescue a soft corner. Your estimate is the lowest of the four, not the average.

Centering: the one you can measure

Measure the left and right borders. The ratio is the bigger border over the total: 3 mm and 2 mm gives 3 ÷ 5 = 60/40. Do the same top/bottom, then flip the card over. The worse axis sets the cap.

Sources disagree on the PSA 10 line

Older copies of PSA's page read "55/45 to 60/40" for a 10 on the front.[3] PSA rewrote the wording in early 2025 without an announcement, and coverage of which way it went is contradictory.[4] psacard.com blocks automated reading, so check it in a browser. The safe working rule: 55/45 or better is a 10 candidate. 56–60 is a long shot at best, so price it as a 9.

Which grader?

GraderKnown for
PSALargest grader and the resale benchmark. Usually earns the biggest premium for the same grade. No subgrades.[6]
BGS (Beckett)Four subgrades (centering, corners, edges, surface) and the Black Label, a 10 on all four.[7] Popular for modern high-end cards.
SGCBlack "tuxedo" slabs, and respected for vintage. Often cheaper.[6]
CGCCame over from comics. It's the main PSA alternative for Pokémon, and it splits the top grade into Pristine 10 and Gem Mint 10.[8]

One thing to know: PSA's parent company, Collectors, has owned SGC since 2024 and bought Beckett in late 2025. All three still grade under their own names.[6][7]

The formula

Expected graded value = (chance of 10 × PSA 10 comp) + (chance of 9 × PSA 9 comp) + (chance of 8 × PSA 8 comp)
Grade it if: expected graded value − (fee + shipping per card) > raw value.

Six points of centering flipped the answer. Card A's centering caps it at 9 (borderline), and at $80 a PSA 9 doesn't cover the fee. Grading pays when the PSA 10 is a real possibility and the 10 is worth far more than the 9. The 4–5 month wait is also a real cost if the player's hype might fade. Think of the Pokémon version: a PSA 10 Charizard is worth sending, but a 9 of a common doesn't even cover the fee.

Real-world rep (15 minutes)

Take three raw cards from your own stack. For each: measure the front and back borders with a ruler (or zoom in on a phone photo), run them through the calculator above, then check corners and surface under a desk lamp. Write down a grade range. Then look up the PSA 10, 9, and raw sold comps on 130point and run the formula. Would you grade any of them at $59.99?
