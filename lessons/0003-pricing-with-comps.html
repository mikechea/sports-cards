Lesson 3 · ~10 minutes

Pricing With Comps

Turn a pile of recent sales into one defensible number, and explain it to the person across the counter.

A customer sets a slabbed rookie on the counter, holds up their phone, and says, "There's one listed on eBay for $400, so that's what it's worth." Your job is to answer with what the card actually sells for, and to show them how you got there. In the hobby, those recent sales are called comps (comparables).

Rule zero: sold, not asking

An active listing is a hope. A sold listing is a fact. One recent pricing guide puts asking prices at 30–50% above what cards actually sell for, and says to work only from sold data.[1] It's the same at a Pokémon show: the sticker on a vendor's Charizard is the opening bid in a conversation, not its value.

One wrinkle: when an eBay seller accepts a Best Offer, the public sold view shows the crossed-out asking price, not the price the buyer paid.[3] If you comp off that crossed-out number, you're comping off an asking price again.

The four-step comp

1 · MATCH THE EXACT CARD

Year, set, card number, parallel, serial numbering, and grade with grading company. A Gold /50, a base Refractor, a PSA 9, and a BGS 9.5 are each a different market. This is Lesson 1's levers in action.

2 · GATHER RECENT SALES

For a $100+ card, pull the last 5–10 sales from roughly the past 30–90 days.[1] Read every title; keyword searches pull in the wrong card.[3]

3 · DROP OUTLIERS, TAKE THE MEDIAN

"Use the median, not the highest or lowest."[1] Be suspicious of a sale far above the rest, especially to a zero-feedback buyer: those are often shill bids meant to pump a price.[9]

4 · CHECK THE TREND, SAY THE NUMBER

If sales are sliding, lean toward the lower recent comps; if rising, toward the median or above.[1] Then explain it in one sentence (below).

Why the median? One fake $400 sale among a handful of $100 sales drags the average way up, but barely moves the median (the middle value when you sort them). Try it: the box below is pre-filled with six made-up sales. Delete the 110 and watch the average move while the median barely does.

The calculator flags sales more than 40% from the median. That's a rule of thumb from one guide, not a standard; Sports Card Investor only drops a sale when there's evidence it wasn't real.[9] Look at a flagged sale before you throw it out.

Two places comps get slippery

Raw vs. graded

Comp graded cards against the same grade from the same company. Comp raw cards against raw sales. A raw card's condition is unknown until it's graded, so never quote a raw card at PSA 10 money. If you only have graded comps for a raw card, you're guessing at its grade (that's Lesson 4).[1]

Thin markets and pop reports

A /10 parallel might sell twice a year. When you can't find 3+ recent sales, widen the window, look at the nearest parallels and grades, and give a range and say it's thin. Don't treat one old sale as gospel.

The population report (PSA's is at psacard.com/pop) counts how many copies a grader has slabbed at each grade.[10] It's useful context, with limits: it covers one grader only, it ignores raw copies, and a low number can just mean nobody bothered to grade a cheap card. It's "a record of what one grading company has certified," not a count of what exists.[11]

Explaining the number at the counter

"That listing at $400 hasn't sold. Here are the last five PSA 10s that did, from $112 to $130. I dropped one $260 sale to a brand-new account because it doesn't match the others. The middle of the real sales is about $120, so that's the market value."

Show the sales, name what you excluded and why, give the number. What the shop then offers is a separate question (Lesson 5).

Real-world rep (10 minutes)

Pick one card you own (or one in a CardVault case next time you visit). Comp it three ways: eBay's Sold Items filter, 130point, and a scan in the eBay app's Price Guide. Write down the clean comps, what you excluded, and your median. Then say your one-sentence explanation out loud as if a customer asked. Did the three tools agree? If not, why?
