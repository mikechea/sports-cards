Lesson 2 · ~10 minutes

The 2026 Product Landscape

When a customer asks "what should I buy?", recommend the right box and explain why.

The Key Holder posting asks for "product knowledge of all products and upcoming trends."[1] On the floor, that question usually arrives as a parent with a ten-year-old saying "he likes baseball, we have about thirty bucks," followed by an adult asking "which box gets me an auto?" They need different answers. To give either one, you need three things: who makes it, what tier it is, and what format it comes in.

1 · Who makes what (October 2026)

The short version: Topps, owned by Fanatics, holds the exclusive license for all three big US leagues. A license is what lets a card show real team logos and uniforms.

LeagueLicensed makerSince
MLBTopps (Fanatics)Topps held MLB exclusively from about 2010. The Fanatics deal took over the league rights in 2026, and Topps still makes the cards.[2]
NBATopps (Fanatics)October 2025, exclusive with the NBA and the players' union (NBPA). It was Topps' first NBA set since 2009-10.[3]
NFLTopps (Fanatics)April 2026, exclusive with the NFL and NFLPA. The first product was 2025 Topps Chrome Football.[4]
What about Panini?

Panini held the NBA license for about 15 years and the NFL license until spring 2026.[13] It still makes cards, but they're unlicensed: no team logos, no team names, and uniforms airbrushed out. Its 2025-26 Prizm Basketball came out July 31, 2026 with no NBA marks,[5] and 2026 Donruss Football is the brand's first ever with no NFL logos.[6] Panini is also suing Fanatics on antitrust grounds. As of August 2026 the case was still in discovery, and a trial is likely in 2027.[7]

Why it matters at the counter: unlicensed cards have generally sold at a discount. Guides cite about 40–60%, but call that figure directional, not measured.[5] Older Prizm cards that were licensed, like a 2024 Silver Prizm rookie, are a different story and are still hobby staples.

2 · The tier ladder

Within one maker, products are stacked by price and by what you get. A single price guide groups them roughly like this:[8]

FLAGSHIP · ~$25–100 / box

Topps Series 1, Series 2, Update (baseball) and Topps Flagship Football. Paper stock, big checklists, lots of cards. Built for kids and set builders.

CHROME · ~$100–400

Topps Chrome, Bowman Chrome, Heritage. Shiny chromium stock, shorter checklists of stars and rookies, and deep refractor parallel ladders.

HIGH-END · ~$300–1,000

Tier One, Museum Collection. Very few cards per box, and nearly every card is an autograph or relic.

ULTRA-PREMIUM · $800+

Topps Dynasty. A handful of numbered patch autos per box. You're paying for one big hit.

Treat those dollar bands (and the format prices below, which are eBay asking prices that often run above list) as rough, since box prices move with demand. The newest flagship is 2026 Topps Flagship Football (released August 21, 2026), Topps' first flagship football set since regaining the NFL license. Fernando Mendoza is on the cover, and each hobby box has one auto or relic.[9]

Bowman and the "1st Bowman"

Bowman is Topps' prospect brand, mostly baseball. A card marked "1st Bowman" is a player's first card in a Bowman product, often issued years before he reaches the majors. It is not a rookie card. The RC comes later, after his MLB debut.[10] A Bowman Chrome 1st autograph is a bet on a minor leaguer, and that's exactly the appeal for prospect collectors.

3 · Box formats: retail vs. hobby

FormatWhere / typical price[11]What you get
HangerBig-box retail, ~$15–25One large pack, often with a format-exclusive parallel
Blaster / "Value box"Retail, ~$25–45A few packs, retail-only parallels, autos possible but not promised
MegaRetail, ~$75–85More packs than a blaster, plus its own exclusive parallels
Hobby boxCard shops and Topps.comBox lists guaranteed hits (e.g. "1 auto or relic," "2 autos")
Jumbo (hobby)Card shopsBigger packs and more guaranteed hits per box

The core difference: retail boxes don't promise autographs, and hobby boxes do, which is mostly what you pay for. Retail is cheaper per card, and the gap is widest on auto-driven products. Hobby Prizm Draft Picks Football ran about $12 per card, against about $1.27 for the mega.[11] Topps now labels its blaster-style box a "Value Box."[11] Always read the box's stated configuration, because "1 auto or relic" is not the same as "1 auto." Even within one product line, a 2026 Bowman hobby box averages one auto and a jumbo averages three.[12]

4 · Match the product to the customer

Ask two questions: "What's your budget?" decides the format. "What are you hoping to get?" decides the tier and brand.

CustomerRecommendWhy
Kid, $30, loves the gameFlagship hanger or value boxLots of cards and familiar players, so the fun is the rip
Wants something shiny to chaseTopps Chrome blaster or megaRefractor parallels at a retail price
Wants a guaranteed autoChrome or Bowman Chrome hobbyHits are printed on the box
Baseball prospect hunterBowman / Bowman Chrome hobby1st Bowman autos of future stars
Wants one specific cardThe single, from the caseBuying it outright is cheaper and more predictable than chasing it in boxes[8]

You already know this from Pokémon

A Target blister pack or Elite Trainer Box is retail: affordable and fun, with no hit promised. A sealed booster box from the game store is the closest thing to hobby, though sports hobby boxes go further and print the guaranteed autos on the box. And just as only The Pokémon Company makes official Pokémon cards, only Topps can put a real NFL, NBA, or MLB logo on a card right now. One Piece works the same way: Bandai is the only maker, a starter deck is the "play tonight" buy, and a booster box is the rip. Lesson 8 covers both.

Real-world rep (10 minutes)

Walk the trading-card aisle at a Target or Walmart, or the sealed-wax shelf at CardVault Palo Alto or SF. Pick three products, and for each say out loud the maker, the tier, the format, and which customer you'd hand it to. Flip each box over and read the configuration line. Does it promise an auto, an "auto or relic," or nothing?
